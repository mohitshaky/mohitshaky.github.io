What actually stops AI reaching production

The demo works, the rollout doesn’t

A notebook proves the idea. Nobody can get it into a Java platform that has tenants, auditors and a budget — and the gap is not model quality.

Security won’t sign it off

Can it read another customer’s documents? What does it do when it does not know? Why did it say that six months ago? A prototype answers none of these.

Token spend has no ceiling

Nobody can say what a bad week costs. In-memory counters give every replica its own budget, which is the same as having none.

The platform underneath is the real blocker

Consumer lag, drifted p99, no tracing. AI features land on top of that and inherit every one of its problems.

Selected casework

Client names are withheld under NDA. What follows is the shape of the problem and the approach taken.

Tier-1 telecom  ·  order management  ·  name under NDA

Orders piled up every time marketing ran a campaign

The problem. A pipeline carrying hundreds of thousands of orders a day, and fine at normal volume. The moment a campaign launched, the order pipeline backed up and support started fixing things by hand.

What I did. Traced the pipeline to find where events were really queueing, rebuilt the consumer groups and partitioning around the correct key, moved slow downstream calls off the poll loop, and added a retry topic so one bad message could no longer block a partition.

Outcome. A pipeline that had been running up to an hour behind at campaign peaks absorbed them without operator intervention. Support stopped clearing stuck orders by hand, and an order that did stall became an alert the team saw before the customer did.

Same pattern, public code: order-lifecycle-bpmn

Banking  ·  account events  ·  name under NDA

Nobody could prove the audit trail was complete

The problem. Answering one compliance question meant querying three stores and reconciling them by hand, with no guarantee the result was right.

What I did. Made consumers idempotent on a stable event ID, made the write path append-only, and split the audit projection from the operational read model so either could be rebuilt without touching the other.

Outcome. Answering one compliance question went from a few hours of engineering time — querying three separate stores and reconciling by hand — to a single query against the audit projection. The trail became provably complete because it was derived from the event log rather than assembled alongside it.

Same pattern, public code: banking-account-service

Telecom  ·  subscriptions  ·  name under NDA

Changing one business rule meant releasing six services

The problem. Subscribe, upgrade, suspend and cancel logic had spread across services over several years. The actual sequence existed only in people’s heads.

What I did. Modelled the lifecycle as an explicit BPMN process so the sequence became something you could read and review, kept the integration event-driven, and made every transition individually traceable.

Outcome. Changing a business rule stopped requiring a coordinated release across six services. It became an edit to a versioned process definition, reviewable in a pull request like any other change.

Common questions

The things people ask me before they get in touch

Your rates are lower than a US contractor’s. What’s the catch?

Cost of living in Gurgaon, and no agency taking a cut. That is the whole of it. The comparison worth making is not my rate against a US contractor’s — it is against the cheap option. A $30/hour developer who needs five hours of your tech lead’s time each week costs you more than a $70/hour engineer who needs one. I am priced where I am because I do not need managing, and because you can read seven years of the relevant work on GitHub before you commit anything.

What happens if you disappear mid-project?

Commits land in your repository from day one, not in a zip at the end, so at any moment you hold everything paid for. Scope is broken into milestones and you pay on acceptance of each, so your exposure is never more than one milestone. And I take two clients at a time specifically so I am not juggling a third when yours needs attention.

What does a typical engagement actually cost end to end?

Most start with the free review or the $950 Health Check. If that turns into work, the two common shapes are Fix & Harden at $2,800 for a single production problem, or a Build at $5,500 for a new service or pipeline — and the Health Check fee comes off either. Teams who want ongoing help move to the $2,900/month fractional arrangement. So realistically: $950 to find out, $2,800–5,500 to fix or build it.

I have never hired an independent consultant before. How does this work?

You tell me what is wrong. We have a short call — free, no sales pitch — and I tell you what I would do and what it would cost. If that sounds right, I write the scope down and you approve it before I start. Nothing is committed until you say so.

Will you work on my existing codebase, or only new projects?

Mostly existing codebases. Almost everything I get called in for is a system somebody else built that is now slow, fragile or hard to change. I do not need it to be tidy, and I will not tell you to rewrite it unless rewriting is genuinely cheaper than fixing it.

Do you sign an NDA?

Routinely. Most of my previous work is under one, which is why the casework above names problems rather than clients. Send yours over and I will sign it before you share any code.

What time zone are you in, and how do we communicate?

Gurgaon, India (IST). I cover a European working day in full, 08:00–12:00 ET for US East Coast teams, and a near-complete shared day with Dubai, Riyadh and Doha — including the Sunday–Thursday Gulf week, when most Western contractors are offline. Slack, Teams, email or your issue tracker, whatever you already use.

You do. Full IP assignment on final payment — code, documentation, configuration. The only carve-out is my own pre-existing reusable libraries, which are licensed to you for the delivered work rather than assigned.
