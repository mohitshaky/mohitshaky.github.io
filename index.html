Selected casework

Client names and system details are withheld under confidentiality. What follows is the shape of the problem and the approach taken.

Telecom  ·  order management

Order orchestration that stalled under campaign load

Situation. A subscriber order pipeline processed events reliably at baseline volume but backed up whenever marketing ran an acquisition campaign. Orders sat in an intermediate state and support handled the fallout manually.

Approach. Traced the pipeline end to end to find where events were actually queueing. Redesigned the consumer group and partition layout around the real partition key, moved blocking downstream calls off the poll loop, and introduced a retry topic with a dead-letter path so a single malformed message could no longer stall a partition.

Result. The pipeline absorbed campaign peaks without operator intervention, and stuck orders became a visible, alertable condition rather than something support discovered from customer complaints.

Banking  ·  account event processing

An audit trail that could not be reconstructed

Situation. Account movements were published as events, but replaying history to answer a compliance question meant querying several stores and reconciling them by hand. Nobody could state with confidence that the trail was complete.

Approach. Established idempotent consumers keyed on a stable event identifier, made the write path append-only, and separated the audit projection from the operational read model so one could be rebuilt without disturbing the other.

Result. History became reconstructible from the event log alone, and compliance queries that previously required engineering time were answered from the projection directly.

Telecom  ·  subscription lifecycle

Lifecycle rules buried across half a dozen services

Situation. Subscribe, upgrade, suspend and cancel logic had accumulated across several services over years. Changing a business rule meant coordinated releases, and the sequence of steps existed only in people's heads.

Approach. Modelled the lifecycle explicitly as a BPMN process so the sequence became a reviewable artefact rather than tribal knowledge, kept service integration event-driven, and made each transition individually observable.

Result. Business rule changes became a process-definition change rather than a multi-service release, and the team could see exactly where any subscription sat in its lifecycle.
