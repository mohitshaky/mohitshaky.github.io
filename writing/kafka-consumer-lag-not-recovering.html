Kafka consumer lag that never recovers

There is a specific, recognisable shape of failure: lag climbs during a traffic peak, the peak passes, and lag stays high. The consumers are running. Nothing is throwing. Throughput just never returns to what it was. That shape narrows the cause down to about four things.

The reason it matters that lag never recovers, rather than simply being high, is that it rules out the obvious answer. If consumers were merely undersized, lag would drain once the burst ended, slowly but steadily. A flat or still-climbing line after the peak means the consumers are not processing at their nominal rate any more — something changed during the burst and stayed changed.

1. Work happening inside the poll loop

This is the most common cause by a wide margin. A consumer polls a batch, and for each record makes a synchronous call — an HTTP request to another service, a database write, an S3 put. Under normal load that call takes 20ms and nobody notices. Under peak load the downstream service slows to 400ms, and now a batch of 500 records takes over three minutes to process.

Two things then happen at once. Obviously, throughput collapses. Less obviously, the consumer misses max.poll.interval.ms, the broker concludes it is dead, and the group rebalances. The partitions move to another consumer, which inherits the same backlog and the same slow downstream call, and misses the deadline too. The group now rebalances continuously and processes almost nothing. That is why lag does not recover: the system has entered a state where rebalancing consumes the time that processing needs.

How to confirm it. Look for these together:

  • Rebalance count climbing during and after the incident, not just at deploys
  • records-lag-max flat rather than draining
  • Downstream p99 latency rising just before consumer lag does — the cause precedes the symptom
  • Log lines about the group coordinator marking members dead

The fix is to get the blocking call off the poll loop. Poll, hand the batch to a bounded worker pool, and commit offsets only after the workers acknowledge. If you cannot restructure quickly, reducing max.poll.records is the emergency lever — smaller batches finish inside the deadline, which at least stops the rebalance storm while you fix the real problem. Raising max.poll.interval.ms is tempting and usually wrong: it hides the failure detection rather than fixing the slowness, and a genuinely stuck consumer now takes far longer to be evicted.

2. Partition skew — one partition holding everything

Kafka parallelism is bounded by partition count, and work is distributed by the partition key. If the key is chosen badly, one partition receives a disproportionate share of traffic and one consumer does most of the work while its peers idle. Aggregate lag looks moderate; per-partition lag shows one line far above the rest.

The classic version is keying by something with a heavy head — a tenant ID where one tenant is ten times larger than the rest, or a country code in a business that is 80% domestic. It is also produced accidentally by a null key with a sticky partitioner, or by a custom partitioner someone wrote years ago for a distribution that no longer exists.

Always graph lag per partition, not per topic. Topic-level lag is an average, and averages hide precisely the failure you are looking for. This one diagnostic change finds more problems than any config tuning.

The fix depends on whether ordering matters. If it does not, key by something with higher cardinality, or use a composite key. If ordering per entity is required — usually the reason the key was chosen in the first place — you cannot simply rekey without breaking the guarantee. In that case the options are splitting the hot entity into sub-keys where its ordering requirement genuinely permits it, or accepting the skew and sizing for the hot partition rather than for the average.

3. Rebalance storms from consumer instability

Every rebalance stops consumption across the whole group. With the default eager assignment strategy, all consumers give up all partitions and re-acquire them. A group rebalancing every thirty seconds spends most of its life not consuming, and lag climbs even though nothing is wrong with the processing code.

Common triggers, in the order I usually find them:

  • Kubernetes liveness probes killing pods during GC pauses or under memory pressure
  • session.timeout.ms set below the actual heartbeat interval under load
  • Autoscaling adding and removing consumers on a metric that oscillates
  • A consumer that OOMs, restarts, consumes, and OOMs again in a loop

The fix is stabilising membership rather than tuning Kafka. Move to CooperativeStickyAssignor so a rebalance no longer stops the entire group — only the moving partitions pause. Then find why members keep leaving. If autoscaling is the trigger, damp the scaling metric or set a longer stabilisation window; a consumer group is not a stateless web tier and should not be scaled like one.

4. A poison message blocking one partition

A single record that always throws on deserialisation or processing, in a consumer that retries indefinitely, stops that partition permanently. Offsets never advance past it. The other partitions drain normally, so aggregate lag looks like it is recovering — while one partition climbs forever.

This is the cause most often missed, because the service looks healthy: it is up, it is consuming, its error rate is a steady trickle rather than a spike. The only clear signal is a single partition whose committed offset has not moved while its log-end offset keeps rising.

The fix is a retry topic and a dead-letter path, with a bounded number of attempts before the record is moved aside. The thing to get right is that a dead-letter topic nobody monitors is a data-loss mechanism with extra steps — it needs an alert on non-zero depth and someone whose job it is to look.

Working through it in order

When I am called in for this, the sequence is always the same, and the first two steps take about ten minutes:

  1. Graph lag per partition. If one line is far above the others, it is skew or a poison message.
  2. Graph rebalance count over the same window. If it is non-zero outside deploys, membership is unstable and nothing else matters until that is fixed.
  3. Overlay downstream latency. If it rises before consumer lag does, the poll loop is blocked on something external.
  4. Check committed offset movement per partition. A partition whose committed offset is frozen while its end offset climbs is a poison message, regardless of what the error rate says.

Almost every case I have seen resolves to one of these four, and the diagnosis is nearly always faster than the fix. The mistake worth avoiding is reaching for consumer config first — increasing max.poll.interval.ms or adding consumers beyond the partition count feels like progress and changes nothing, because neither addresses why throughput dropped in the first place.
